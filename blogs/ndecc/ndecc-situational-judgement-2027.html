NDECC Situational Judgement 2027: Tasks, Preparation & Scoring

The NDECC Situational Judgement component tests how an internationally trained dentist manages realistic problems that arise in Canadian dental practice. It is not a conventional multiple-choice test. Under the latest published NDECC Protocol, candidates complete 10 stations: five written-answer stations and five conversation stations with an actor.

As of 17 November 2026, the latest NDEB protocol is the 2026 NDECC Protocol effective June 15, 2026. NDEB has not published a separate 2027 protocol, so this is the current official structure for 2027 planning unless NDEB replaces it before your sitting.

Quick Answers

  • How many Situational Judgement stations are there? 10.
  • How are they divided? Five written-answer stations and five conversation stations with an actor.
  • How long is the component? Half a day.
  • How many competency areas are tested? Five.
  • How many stations must you pass? At least six of 10.
  • Is six passes enough by itself? No. You must also pass at least one station in each of the five competency areas.
  • Can you fail both stations in one competency area? No. That fails the Situational Judgement component even if your total station passes are otherwise high enough.

The 10-Station Structure

The current protocol requires 10 Situational Judgement stations.

They are divided into:

  • 5 written-answer stations
  • 5 conversation stations with an actor

The component is scheduled for half a day and may occur in the morning or afternoon.

Your Clinical Skills component can be on the other examination day, so candidates must keep the entire two-day NDECC period available.

All Situational Judgement stations have the same duration under the current protocol.

There are no scheduled breaks during the component. A candidate may request a washroom break if needed, but time can be lost from a station and no extra time is added.

The Five Competency Requirements

There are five requirement areas, with two stations per requirement.

Requirement areaExamples of what can be assessed
Patient-Centered CareIdentifying problems, diagnosis, sequencing care, treatment planning, interpreting history
ProfessionalismEthical dilemmas, referrals, professional behaviour and responsible use of social media
Communication and CollaborationPatient-centred communication, consent, privacy, histories, referrals, staff interaction
Practice and Information ManagementRecords, protocols, evidence-based decisions and practice-management issues
Health PromotionCaries risk, prevention, access to care, social determinants and patient advocacy

The protocol makes clear that these examples are not an exhaustive list.

The goal is to assess how you manage a practice-related situation, not how well you memorize one script.

What Can Happen at a Station?

Depending on the station, the task can involve:

  • writing an answer;
  • typing an answer;
  • speaking with a simulated patient or other person;
  • reviewing a video;
  • interpreting information supplied in the scenario;
  • completing another practice-related task relevant to the competency being assessed.

Each station starts with a scenario and a specific task.

Your answer needs to address the task that was actually asked. Giving a polished speech that does not solve the station problem is not enough.

All stations are video and/or voice recorded under the current protocol.

Written Stations vs Conversation Stations

Written-answer stations

Written stations assess whether you can identify the important problem, organize an appropriate response and include the required actions or considerations.

Strong answers are usually:

  • direct;
  • complete;
  • organized;
  • based on Canadian standards and current evidence;
  • focused on the specific problem rather than generic dentistry.

Conversation stations

Conversation stations involve an actor and test what you do and communicate in the scenario.

This can include:

  • explaining treatment options;
  • obtaining informed consent;
  • addressing concerns;
  • demonstrating empathy;
  • communicating risk;
  • managing ethical or professional issues;
  • collaborating appropriately.

Do not confuse “speaking a lot” with competence. The grading is based on required behaviours and acceptable management of the scenario.

How the Stations Are Graded

Each station is graded Pass or Fail.

The protocol describes performance using three levels:

  • Competent
  • Minimally Competent
  • Not Competent

Competent and minimally competent performance can pass a station.

Not competent performance fails it.

For the official sample rubric, NDEB explains that the required minimally competent behaviours must be demonstrated to pass. Additional optimal behaviours can show stronger performance, but they do not replace a missing required behaviour.

A critical or not-competent behaviour can fail the station.

This is why checklist-style preparation can be useful only if you understand the underlying clinical and professional reasoning. Memorizing phrases without recognizing the required actions is risky.

Exact Passing Rule

The current protocol gives a two-part passing standard:

  1. Pass at least six of the 10 stations.
  2. Pass at least one of the two stations in each of the five requirement areas.

Both conditions must be met.

Example:

  • You pass 7 of 10 stations.
  • But you fail both Professionalism stations.

You still fail the Situational Judgement component because one requirement area has no passing station.

NDEB reaffirmed this structure during its NDECC consultation review because each competency area represents an important domain of safe Canadian dental practice.

Why the Five Areas Matter

The five requirements are not arbitrary topic labels.

Together they test whether a beginning practitioner can:

  • keep the patient at the centre of care;
  • make professionally defensible decisions;
  • communicate and collaborate;
  • manage information and practice responsibilities;
  • promote health and access to care.

A candidate can therefore be clinically knowledgeable yet still fail if they repeatedly mishandle consent, privacy, communication, ethical obligations or practice-management decisions.

A Better Preparation Method

Step 1: Learn the five requirement areas

You should be able to recognize whether a scenario primarily concerns:

  • patient-centred care;
  • professionalism;
  • communication/collaboration;
  • practice/information management;
  • health promotion.

Step 2: Practise identifying required actions

For each scenario ask:

  • What is the immediate problem?
  • Is there a patient-safety issue?
  • What information must be gathered?
  • Is consent required?
  • Is confidentiality involved?
  • Does the patient need options explained?
  • Is documentation required?
  • Is referral or escalation appropriate?
  • Is there an ethical or legal issue?
  • What follow-up is necessary?

Step 3: Practise both formats

Do not prepare only spoken OSCE-style responses.

You need to be comfortable with both written and actor-based stations.

Step 4: Use Canadian standards, not memorized cultural assumptions

NDECC assesses entry-to-practice competence in Canada.

Review current Canadian professional standards, ethics, communication expectations, consent, privacy, record keeping and evidence-based care.

Common Mistakes

  • Preparing for five stations instead of the current 10.
  • Assuming all stations are conversations with actors.
  • Forgetting that there are two stations per competency area.
  • Thinking six total passes guarantee an overall pass.
  • Memorizing scripts without adapting to the actual task.
  • Ignoring documentation, consent or privacy in clinically focused scenarios.
  • Giving every possible answer instead of making a clear professional decision.
  • Using reconstructed or recalled exam content rather than official preparation resources.

What Happens If You Fail Situational Judgement?

If you pass Clinical Skills and fail Situational Judgement, current NDEB rules require you to repeat only the Situational Judgement component.

If you fail both components, you repeat the full NDECC.

NDECC attempts remain subject to the current 60-month period from first passing ACJ.

See NDECC Results 2027 and NDECC Failed Component Retake Strategy.

FAQs

1. Is NDECC Situational Judgement an MCQ exam?

No. The current protocol uses five written-answer stations and five conversation stations with an actor.

2. How many stations do I need to pass?

At least six of 10, plus at least one station in every one of the five requirement areas.

3. Are all stations the same length?

The current protocol says all stations have the same duration.

4. Can I use notes during the component?

The current exam-day rules prohibit bringing ordinary personal materials into the Situational Judgement testing area. Follow the latest protocol and test-centre instructions.

5. If I fail Situational Judgement but pass Clinical Skills, what do I repeat?

Only the failed Situational Judgement component.
