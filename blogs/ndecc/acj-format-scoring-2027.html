The Assessment of Clinical Judgement (ACJ) is the second examination in the NDEB Equivalency Process. It is designed to test whether a dentist can interpret clinical information, formulate diagnoses, make treatment decisions and interpret radiographs at the level expected of a beginning practitioner in Canada.

For 2027 candidates, the latest official governing document is still the 2026 ACJ Protocol, effective January 19, 2026. NDEB has not published a separate 2027 ACJ protocol. The current protocol describes a computer-based examination of 120–150 single-answer and multiple-answer multiple-choice questions, completed in 5.5 hours with one scheduled 30-minute break.

Current Protocol Status for 2027

NDEB's Exam Resources page currently lists the 2026 ACJ Protocol – Revised.

That protocol is effective January 19, 2026 and remains the current official source for 2027 planning unless NDEB replaces it before your examination.

Candidates should also use:

  • ACJ Sample Questions and Answers;
  • AFK and ACJ Electronic Exam Orientation;
  • NDEB Reference Texts;
  • the KSAs for a Beginning Dental Practitioner in Canada.

Do not rely on an unofficial “2027 ACJ syllabus” if it conflicts with the current protocol.

The exam is case-based. Questions can include case histories, dental charts, clinical images and radiographs.

What the ACJ Actually Assesses

The protocol separates ACJ content into two broad kinds of judgement.

Diagnosis, treatment planning and clinical decision-making

Questions can require you to:

  • identify the most likely diagnosis;
  • determine immediate management;
  • choose the most appropriate management;
  • sequence treatment;
  • interpret medical and dental history;
  • use periodontal and other clinical charting;
  • combine clinical findings with imaging.

This is why ACJ preparation should not be reduced to memorizing isolated facts.

Radiographic interpretation

Questions can assess your ability to:

  • prescribe appropriate radiographic imaging;
  • assess image quality and findings;
  • interpret radiographic appearances;
  • establish diagnoses from radiographs.

NDEB's current protocol explicitly integrates radiographic interpretation throughout the examination.

Current ACJ Blueprint

The 2026 protocol publishes approximate percentages for six major content areas.

Content areaApproximate percentage
Oral Medicine/Oral Pathology, Oral Surgery25 ± 5%
Pharmacotherapeutics, Medically Complex Patients, Medical Emergencies, Geriatrics, Local Anesthesia22 ± 5%
Cariology/Restorative Dentistry18 ± 5%
Periodontics15 ± 5%
Endodontics10 ± 5%
Orthodontics, Pediatric Dentistry10 ± 5%

These percentages are approximate. They are a blueprint, not a promise of an exact number of questions in your sitting.

The largest areas are oral medicine/oral surgery and the pharmacotherapeutics/medically-complex-patient cluster, but every listed area can materially affect your result.

Single-Answer Questions

Single-answer questions use the instruction to select one correct answer.

These are usually the most straightforward structurally, but the clinical reasoning may still be complex because the answer depends on the entire case.

Read carefully for wording such as:

  • most likely diagnosis;
  • immediate management;
  • most appropriate management;
  • next step.

The current ACJ protocol defines these terms because they can change what counts as the best answer.

Multiple-Answer Questions

Some ACJ questions instruct you to select one or more correct answers.

The current protocol uses a scoring method where individual response options can carry different values.

The practical rule is important:

  • selecting all correct answers can earn the full mark;
  • selecting only some correct answers can earn partial credit in some situations;
  • selecting an incorrect answer can reduce the total for that question to zero.

All questions have a maximum score of 1 and a minimum of 0.

This means indiscriminately selecting extra choices is risky.

Case Histories and Periodontal Charts

The ACJ protocol shows how patient case histories can be presented, including:

  • chief complaint;
  • dental history;
  • medical history;
  • medications and allergies;
  • social/family history;
  • clinical findings;
  • additional images or charts.

Periodontal charting can include:

  • probing depths;
  • recession;
  • bleeding on probing;
  • mobility;
  • furcation involvement;
  • missing teeth and implants.

You need to be comfortable reading chart information quickly rather than learning the notation on exam day.

Tooth Numbering

NDEB uses the FDI two-digit tooth-numbering system in its examinations.

If you normally use Universal numbering, convert that habit before the ACJ.

Misreading a tooth number can turn a correct clinical concept into a wrong exam answer.

Scoring and the Pass Standard

For result reporting, verification and retake decisions, use ACJ Results 2027.

ACJ uses a test-equating and re-scaling process.

The current passing standard is:

75 or greater = Pass

A score below 75 is reported as Fail, and unsuccessful candidates receive their test-equated, re-scaled score.

A passing candidate receives a Pass result rather than a detailed subject-by-subject performance report.

NDEB states that no further breakdown of ACJ results is available.

Why 75 Does Not Mean 75%

NDEB explicitly states that the rescaled score is not the percentage of questions answered correctly.

The exam uses test equating to account for differences in difficulty between examination forms.

Therefore:

  • 75 does not mean exactly 75% correct;
  • 74 does not necessarily mean you were one question short;
  • another candidate's recollection of raw performance cannot be converted reliably into your ACJ score.

The examination is also not graded on a curve.

See ACJ Results 2027 for result interpretation.

Results Timing

The current ACJ protocol says results are normally released within eight weeks of the examination.

You receive an email notification when the result is available in your online profile.

Results are not released by telephone, email or fax.

This is more precise than relying on social-media estimates, but “normally within eight weeks” is still not the same as a guaranteed fixed release date.

Appeals and Score Verification

If you receive a failing grade, the current protocol gives 30 days from the result-release date to request verification of your score.

Verification checks the recorded score; it does not provide access to examination questions or answer sheets.

For broader rules and the current verification fee, see ACJ Results 2027.

Maximum Attempts

After an ACJ pass, the next stage is NDECC Registration 2027; the NDEB Equivalency Timeline 2027 explains the 60-month clock.

The ACJ can be taken a maximum of three times.

This makes preparation quality important after an unsuccessful attempt. A near-pass score is not a reason to repeat the same strategy unchanged.

After passing ACJ, you become eligible for NDECC and the current 60-month NDECC completion period begins from the date you first pass ACJ.

See NDECC Registration 2027.

A Better Preparation Strategy

Candidates coming from AFK can compare the change in assessment style with AFK 2027 Format and Syllabus and AFK Results 2027.

1. Learn the blueprint before choosing resources

Use the official content distribution to check whether your study plan is over-weighting one discipline.

2. Practise multi-answer questions carefully

Do not use a “select everything that sounds possible” approach.

For each option, decide whether it is genuinely correct in the context of the case.

3. Practise case integration

ACJ is not an AFK-style broad knowledge recall exam.

Train with cases that combine:

  • history;
  • clinical findings;
  • imaging;
  • diagnosis;
  • management.

4. Review radiographs routinely

Radiographic interpretation is integrated into the exam purpose and content.

5. Use official sample material

NDEB specifically recommends its ACJ Sample Questions and Answers and the Electronic Exam Orientation.

Use third-party material only as supplementary practice, not as a substitute for the current protocol.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating ACJ like a pure recall exam.
  • Assuming “120–150” means every administration has exactly 150 questions.
  • Treating 75 as 75% correct.
  • Guessing extra options on multi-answer questions without considering the scoring risk.
  • Ignoring radiographic interpretation.
  • Using Universal numbering instead of FDI.
  • Waiting until exam day to learn the Prometric interface.
  • Relying on recalled or reconstructed NDEB examination content.
